I've been thinking about a deceptively simple question lately:

**What is a business?**

Ask an accountant, and it's a financial entity.

Ask a lawyer, and it's a legal entity.

Ask an operations manager, and it's a collection of processes.

Ask a software engineer, and it's a set of systems.

Ask a marketer, and it's a brand.

All of those are true.

But I wonder if there's another way to think about it.

> **A business is a predictive model of its customers.**

Every pricing decision.

Every sales process.

Every warehouse.

Every hiring plan.

Every dashboard.

Every KPI.

Every product roadmap.

Each one encodes a set of hypotheses about customer behaviour.

Who buys?

When?

Why?

How often?

At what price?

Through which channel?

The business doesn't just sell products.

It continuously bets that its understanding of customer behaviour is accurate enough to make good decisions.

Which leads to an interesting consequence.

If customer behaviour changes...

...then the business model isn't the only thing that needs to adapt.

The organization's *mental model* needs to adapt too.

Maybe that's why concepts like data quality, concept drift, MLOps, experimentation, organizational incentives, AI, and strategy feel so deeply connected.

They're all mechanisms for answering the same question:

> **"Do we still understand the world well enough to make good decisions?"**

I'm exploring this idea in a longer essay, along with what machine learning, cognitive science, and systems thinking can teach us about building organizations that learn.
